Pay More, Get Less?

How to use
  • Point at a year, a game or a price band to highlight the matching games in the other charts.
  • Click it (or press Enter or Space) to keep the selection. Click again or press Esc to clear.
  • Keyboard: Tab moves between charts, arrow keys move between marks inside a chart.
  • r in chart 1 is Pearson’s correlation between price and (log) peak players: 0 means no link, 1 a perfect one.
  • Review tier and Developer buttons (click to hide or show) filter every chart: the lines, dots, correlation and review shares are recalculated. A hidden review tier turns hollow in chart 3. Axes stay fixed so the charts can be compared before and after.

The verdict

Paying more didn't buy more players or better reviews. Median prices doubled, but price barely tracks peak players (r = 0.30), and the share of Overwhelmingly Positive games falls from 70% under $15 to 14% at $50 and up. The best-loved games are mostly cheap, and often indie.

Why might that be? A cheap game only reaches Steam's top 200 if people love it, while a $70 blockbuster can get there on brand and marketing. So this is a pattern among popular games, not proof that a higher price causes worse reviews.

About this dashboard: data, limits and encodings

Data and limits

  • Top 200 Steam games by owner estimate; 58 free games removed, leaving 142 paid games.
  • Price is the list price, not the sale price on the day of the crawl (41 games were discounted).
  • Chart 2 covers 2012 to 2024, where every year has at least 5 games. 23 games from other years and 1 with no recorded year appear only in charts 1 and 3.
  • Indie means the game carries Steam's Indie tag. One game has no genres recorded and is drawn as a square.
  • This is a sample of popular games, so prices run higher than Steam as a whole. All 22 games at $50 and up are non-indie.
